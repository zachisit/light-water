Chips Broke First

On Thursday, November 5, the stocks behind the artificial-intelligence boom had one of their worst days on record. The selling started in chipmakers in Asia overnight, reached every part of the U.S. stock market after the open, and was still going at the closing bell. Bonds, the dollar and corporate credit hardly moved.

Market Note
The read: Two days after the U.S. midterm elections, investors sold the companies that make the chips and equipment for AI data centers. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12.2%. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52%, its biggest one-day drop in 19 months, Reuters reported, and the S&P 500 fell 4.36%. Reuters tied the selling to worries that Democrats’ election gains could bring tighter rules for AI and data centers. Nothing of the kind has been enacted. The selling followed a weak night for chip stocks in Seoul, Tokyo and Taipei and some disappointing earnings. Treasury bonds, the dollar and high-yield credit barely moved, so this looks like investors marking down one popular trade, not a scramble for safety across all markets.

A few terms, for readers outside finance
An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a basket of stocks, bonds or commodities that trades like a single share. SPY tracks the S&P 500; SMH and SOXX hold chipmakers; XLK holds large technology companies. Semiconductors are computer chips, and the companies that design them, make them and build the machines that make them. The AI spenders, sometimes called hyperscalers, are the cloud giants (Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta) that buy those chips by the hundreds of thousands to fill data centers. The VIX uses S&P 500 option prices to estimate how much the index is expected to swing over the next 30 days; it rises when investors are nervous. High-yield bonds are loans to riskier companies; when investors fear a real economic or financial crisis, they usually fall hard.

The Day Before: A Split

The midterm elections were held on Tuesday, November 3. On Wednesday the market split in two. The AI spenders rallied: Microsoft rose 6.7% and Amazon 6.1%. The companies that supply them fell. Micron, which makes memory chips, dropped 7.0%, and Lam Research, which makes chipmaking equipment, fell 4.0%. Nvidia lost 3.2%.

Reuters later described the worry behind that split. Investors feared that Democratic wins would slow the build-out of data centers, the warehouse-sized buildings full of AI chips. It cited a pledge by Texas governor-elect Gina Hinojosa to halt new data-center construction, and calls from Democrats in Congress for laws to protect consumers from the electricity costs of data centers and to require independent testing of AI systems, according to the report. These are pledges and proposals, not law. Masahiko Loo of State Street said Democratic control raised scrutiny of data centers, power demand and consumer costs, but noted that President Trump keeps his veto, Reuters reported. His read was more political risk, not necessarily an end to the spending.

The logic of the split is straightforward. If data centers get harder or slower to build, the companies paying for them spend less, which can look like good news for their profit margins. The companies selling them chips and machines lose orders. Chip stocks had also run very far: the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index went into Election Day up 46% from a year earlier, the strongest run into a midterm in the Motley Fool’s eight-cycle sample, the publication noted. A group that has risen that much has further to fall when the story is questioned.

Overnight: Asia Sold Chips First

The selling picked up in Asia before Americans woke up. In Seoul, the Kospi fell 5.28%. Samsung Electronics lost 7.57% and SK hynix, a leading maker of the high-bandwidth memory used in AI chips, 9.46%. The exchange briefly paused program trading, and Yonhap linked the selling to AI-regulation worries after the U.S. elections. In Tokyo, chip-test maker Advantest fell 14.7% and equipment maker Tokyo Electron 9.4%. The Nikkei, which they weigh on heavily, fell 1.8%, while the broader Topix rose 1.2%, Reuters reported. Taiwan’s Taiex fell 3.2%, according to Taiwan’s Central News Agency. In Europe, Dutch chip-equipment maker ASML was down 2.7% shortly after the open while the wider market rose, Reuters reported; it closed down 6.6%.

There was company news, too. After the U.S. close on Wednesday, Qualcomm missed estimates for smartphone-chip sales and its shares fell more than 6% in after-hours trading, Reuters reported. Arm, whose chip designs sit inside most phones and a growing share of data-center processors, beat on total revenue but missed on royalties, $670 million against $720.8 million expected; its shares fell about 3.5% after hours, according to Reuters. Early Thursday, Reuters relayed a Bloomberg report that Micron had paused work on a $4.5 billion plant in Taiwan amid a labor dispute; Reuters said it could not immediately verify the report. Later in the morning, Reuters reported, citing two people familiar with the matter, that about 600,000 Nvidia H200 chips were sitting in Hong Kong while Beijing withheld import approval.

None of these stories alone explains a 12% fall in a whole industry, and we did not find a single headline that started it. What the record shows is a sector already under pressure on Wednesday, weak overnight trading in the same companies across Asia and Europe, and a few pieces of bad news landing at the same time.

U.S. trading before the open shows the same thing. Around 4:00 a.m. Eastern, the chip fund SMH was already down about 2% from Wednesday’s close; by 9:25 a.m. it was down 3.9%. The S&P 500 fund SPY was roughly flat the whole time, and the consumer staples fund XLP, which holds companies like Procter & Gamble and Coca-Cola, was up about 1%. At the opening bell this was a problem for chip stocks, not yet for the whole market.

After the Open: Everything Went

SPY’s highest price of the day came in its first five minutes, and even that was below Wednesday’s close. From there it fell in steps, with each bounce weaker than the last: down 1.3% by 9:45, 1.7% by 10:00, 2.5% by 10:30 and 3.2% by 11:00. The steepest five-minute drops came on heavy trading. The selling had moved beyond chips. The small-company fund IWM, flat at the open, was down 2.9% by 11:00, and the financial-sector fund XLF went from a 1.3% gain at the 9:30 open to a 1.6% loss.

By the close, every one of the eleven S&P 500 sector funds was down, and every one finished below its average trading price for the day. Technology and chips fell most, on the heaviest volume.

Chipmakers Were the Target; the Spenders Were Collateral

The single stocks show where the selling was aimed. The companies that make and equip AI chips fell 9% to 17% on close to three times their normal volume. The cloud giants that buy those chips fell 2% to 4% on lighter volume.

AMD stands out. It reported record quarterly revenue of $13.4 billion, up 45%, on Tuesday, November 3, according to its filing. On the earnings call, chief executive Lisa Su forecast more than 80% revenue growth in 2027. Its shares jumped about 8% after hours, Reuters reported. Two days later they fell 17%. A drop that large so soon after strong results is consistent with investors reassessing the whole industry’s outlook rather than the company’s latest quarter, though the price move alone cannot tell us their reasons.

Micron had its own problems. By Thursday’s close it had fallen about 33% in six sessions. Press coverage pointed to rising competition from Chinese memory maker CXMT, citing figures from the trade publication DigiTimes that we could not independently verify, and there was the reported pause at its Taiwan plant.

After 11 a.m., Even the Safe Places Were Sold

Through the morning, investors moved money into the parts of the market that usually hold up in a downturn. At 11:00, the consumer staples fund XLP was up 1.6% and the utilities fund XLU was up 0.5%, even as SPY was down 3.2%. That did not last. Both turned lower before noon and closed down about 0.7%; XLP finished 3 cents above its low of the day.

This is the clearest sign in the day’s trading that the selling went beyond a view on chips. When defensive stocks that were rising get sold too, it is often because investors are cutting their overall exposure to stocks and selling what they can, including their winners. Price data cannot prove that, and we have no data on who was selling. But it fits the pattern.

The Close: Selling Into the Bell

A quarter of SPY’s regular-session volume traded in the last hour, against 8% to 9% in each hour around midday. Its single busiest five-minute stretch of the day was the last one, from 3:55 to 4:00 p.m., and SPY fell during it. SPY closed at $733.00, just above its low of $732.71. The Nasdaq 100 fund QQQ’s official close, $647.05, was its low of the day.

Heavy selling into the close, with prices finishing at or near the lows, usually means the selling was not finished when the market shut. That is a description of Thursday, not a forecast for Friday. Days like this are often followed by large moves in either direction.

What Did Not Happen

For a 4% drop in the S&P 500, the bond, currency and credit markets we track were quiet.

  • Bonds did not panic. The long-term Treasury fund TLT rose 0.2%. In a true flight to safety, investors usually pile into Treasuries.
  • The dollar did not jump. The dollar fund UUP was up 0.1%.
  • Credit barely moved. The high-yield bond fund HYG fell 0.3% while stocks fell 4.4%. Riskier corporate debt usually sells off with stocks when the worry is a recession or a funding squeeze. It did not here.
  • Oil and gold rose. The oil fund USO gained 1.9% and the gold fund GLD 0.8%. An oil rally is not what a sudden recession scare usually looks like.
  • Fear was concentrated in stocks. The VIX jumped 70%, from 16.61 to 28.18, its highest close since March 30.

Put together, Thursday looks like a repricing of one very popular trade, AI chip stocks, that spread to the rest of the stock market as investors cut risk. It does not look like a crisis in the economy or the financial system. That reading could change if credit starts to weaken in the days ahead.

A Fear Gauge Running Ahead of Itself

One detail in the VIX is worth a closer look. The VIX measures expected S&P 500 volatility over the next 30 days. VIX futures let traders bet on where that gauge will be at later dates, and most of the time they trade above it. When the VIX jumps above every futures contract, the market is showing much more stress right now than it expects at later dates.

Tom McClellan of McClellan Financial has written about this using VIX futures. He measures the gap between the VIX and its highest-priced futures contract, and argues that when the VIX rises above all of its futures, it has been a good sign of a “bottoming condition” for stocks. He adds an important caveat: a bottoming condition “is not necessarily the same thing as the exact bottom moment.” At the April 2025 tariff low, his measure reached −30%.

Thursday met that condition. The VIX closed at 28.18. Every VIX futures contract settled below it, from 24.54 for November to between 21.3 and 21.6 for contracts expiring next spring and summer, according to Cboe’s daily settlement prices. Using those settlements, the gap between the highest-priced monthly contract and the VIX, as a share of the VIX, was −12.9%. That is the deepest reading since April 2025. By the same calculation, it was −11.4% on November 20, 2025, the reading he wrote about, and −29.4% on April 7, 2025, near that spring’s low. Cboe’s longer-dated volatility indexes tell the same story: the VIX closed above its 3-month (25.55), 6-month (25.29) and 1-year (24.60) counterparts.

The futures market was saying that Thursday’s fear was extreme relative to what traders expect over the coming months. In McClellan’s reading, that is what a bottoming condition looks like. It describes how stretched fear was at Thursday’s close. It is not a forecast of when the selling will stop, and his own caveat applies.

What We Can’t Tell Yet

This note is built from one day of U.S. market data and from news reports. We could not identify a single trigger for the overnight selling, and we found no new export rule, permitting rule or announced cut in AI spending on November 4 or 5. Most of the policy risk in the reporting is pledges and proposals, not law, and President Trump retains his veto. Several of the company stories, including the Micron plant and the Nvidia shipments, rest on unnamed sources and were not confirmed by the companies when we wrote this. We have no data on who was selling, so our reading of the afternoon as investors cutting exposure is an inference from prices, not a measurement.

What to Watch

Nvidia reports quarterly results on Tuesday, November 17, the company said. It is the biggest single test of whether AI spending is holding up. Before that, watch three things. First, whether chip stocks hold Thursday’s lows on lighter volume, which would suggest the heaviest selling is done. Second, whether high-yield bonds stay calm; a sharp drop in HYG would change the picture from a stock-market repricing to something broader. Third, what the incoming Congress and state governments actually propose on data centers and AI, as opposed to what was said on election night.
