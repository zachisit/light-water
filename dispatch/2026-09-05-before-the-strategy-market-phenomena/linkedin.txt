COMPANY ACCOUNT - Light Water Capital
========================================================================
Shardul Mehetar, our quantitative strategy intern, spent last week on a question we don't usually ask directly: are the market behaviours our strategies are built on top of actually there when you test them on their own terms, rather than checking whether something built on them happened to make money?

He took seven of them across US equities back to 2004 - momentum, volatility clustering, shock response, volume, resiliency, commonality and correlation - and tested each against its own null hypothesis.

Two methodological corrections accounted for most of what came back.

The first was to shuffle each stock's history within that stock, which keeps every observation but destroys the ordering. If an effect survives that, it can't be about timing, because there is no timing left in the data; it is just a description of what the stock is generally like. Applied to volatility clustering, between 70% and 87% of the effect survived, depending on the horizon, which means most of what looked like memory was really a fairly stable ranking of which names are jumpy.

The second was to account for overlapping windows. A 120-day rolling correlation calculated today shares 119 of its 120 days with the one calculated yesterday, so consecutive readings are nowhere near independent, and treating them as if they were overstates how much evidence you actually have. Of 155 tests of the claim that correlation and dispersion persist, none survived the correction. In one case the same statistic over the same window moved from p = 0.000 to p = 0.888.

What did hold up was the gap between variance and direction. Depending on the horizon, variance came out between 23 and 354 times more predictable, which is a large part of why volatility targeting tends to survive contact with live markets while direction filters often don't.

The write-up also includes the eighteen defects he found in our own tooling along the way. Ten of them would have returned a plausible-looking wrong answer without raising an error, and that is the category we find hardest to defend against.

PERSONAL REPOST CAPTION
========================================================================
I spent last week not testing a single strategy, and instead testing the seven market behaviours that strategies tend to assume are true, each one against its own null hypothesis.

The piece of method I'll carry forward is the within-stock shuffle: reorder a stock's history at random so that every observation survives but the sequence is gone. Whatever is still predictive afterwards cannot be about timing. When I ran that against volatility clustering, most of the effect came through intact, which sounds like good news until you realise it means the signal was mostly telling me which names are generally jumpy rather than when any of them would be.

The correlation work went the other way. Once I corrected for the fact that consecutive rolling windows share almost all of their data, none of the 155 persistence results held up, and I had to withdraw findings I had already written up.

I also logged eighteen defects in my own tooling, ten of which would have handed me a confident wrong answer with no error raised. Writing all of that up, including the parts that didn't survive, took longer than the analysis did and was probably the more useful half of the week.
