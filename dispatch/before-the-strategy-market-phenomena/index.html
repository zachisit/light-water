Before the Strategy

Seven market phenomena that form the backbone of quantitative research - and why understanding why a market effect exists should come before trying to trade it.

LW
Light Water Capital
Research

At Light Water, one question has increasingly shaped the way we approach quantitative research:

Before asking whether a strategy works, do we actually understand the market phenomenon the strategy is trying to exploit?

That distinction sounds simple. In practice, it changes almost everything.

A conventional quantitative workflow can begin with an idea: momentum, mean reversion, unusual volume, a volatility filter, a breakout, a shock-recovery pattern. The researcher defines some rules, backtests them, adjusts parameters, adds filters, measures Sharpe ratios and drawdowns, and eventually decides whether the strategy appears promising.

There is nothing inherently wrong with that process.

The problem is that a profitable-looking backtest does not necessarily tell us why it worked.

Was it exploiting a persistent market state?

A permanent characteristic of the securities selected?

A particular historical regime?

A universe-construction artifact?

Overlapping observations?

Volatility exposure?

A market-wide movement disguised as stock-specific information?

An estimator that accidentally incorporates information it should not have?

Or simply a sufficiently large search over specifications that eventually produced something attractive?

A backtest can answer:

“What would have happened?”

But serious research must eventually answer a harder question:

“What exactly are we measuring, and why should it have happened at all?”

That is the motivation behind a research program we have been building at Light Water around a set of foundational market phenomena.

Seven modules have now been executed across U.S. equities. Six are closed; the seventh remains intentionally held behind an external review gate.

The purpose was not to discover seven trading strategies.

It was to investigate seven pieces of market behavior that sit underneath an enormous number of quantitative strategies.

Those seven are:

  1. Continuation and Reversal: When does past price movement continue, and when does it reverse?
  2. Volatility Clustering: When instability appears, how much of it actually persists?
  3. Jumps and Shocks: After an unusually large move, what changes?
  4. Volume and Liquidity: What does trading activity tell us about what happens next?
  5. Market Resiliency and Impact Decay: After displacement, what comes back and what remains moved?
  6. Common vs. Idiosyncratic Movement: Is a stock moving because of the market, or because of itself?
  7. Correlation, Dispersion and Crowding: How does the market behave when securities increasingly move together?

Taken separately, these are familiar concepts.

Taken together, they begin to form something much more useful:

A framework for investigating market behavior before converting it into a strategy.

Why start with phenomena instead of strategies?

Consider a momentum strategy.

At first glance, momentum seems straightforward: securities that performed well continue performing well.

But underneath that statement are several unanswered questions.

Which lookback horizon?

Which forward horizon?

Does momentum exist across the entire return distribution, or only among extreme winners?

Does it survive during stressed markets?

Does it depend on volatility?

Does a smooth trend behave differently from a noisy trend?

Does the effect come from market-driven movement or company-specific movement?

Has its behavior changed over twenty years?

Once those questions are asked, “momentum” stops being one phenomenon.

It becomes a conditional map.

The same is true of mean reversion, volatility strategies, liquidity signals, event-driven strategies and regime models.

That is why we wanted to work one layer below the strategy.

Instead of starting with:

“Can we make money from this?”

we started with:

“What is actually true about this?”

Only after answering the second question should the first become interesting.

The research foundation

The program uses point-in-time constituents of the S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 and generally spans approximately 2004/2005 through 2026, depending on the module.

Individual investigations typically contain roughly 2.4 to 2.6 million date-security observations.

The underlying frozen security corpus contains 36,684 securities, including 20,911 delisted names carried through their actual final observations.

That last detail matters far more than it sounds.

If we construct a historical universe using only companies that survive today, then companies that disappeared, failed, merged or were removed from an index disappear from history as well.

That does not merely make the sample slightly cleaner.

It can manufacture conclusions.

We saw exactly that happen.

In our investigation of jumps and shocks, an apparent long-run structural change suggested that post-shock behavior had become dramatically stronger over time.

After rebuilding the comparison correctly, the entire trend disappeared.

The market behavior had not changed.

The universe had.

That finding alone reinforced one of the principles behind the entire program:

Good quantitative research is not only about computing the correct statistic. It is about making sure the statistic answers the question you think you asked.

Phenomenon 1: Continuation and Reversal

We began with perhaps the most fundamental question in systematic equity research:

Does past return predict future return?

Instead of assuming one answer, we created a return-memory map.

Past-return windows of 1, 5, 10, 20, 60, 120 and 252 trading days were crossed with forward horizons of the same lengths.

That produced a 7×7 map.

And the first important lesson appeared almost immediately:

There is no single “momentum versus mean-reversion” answer.

There are several different regions.

Very short horizons: reversal dominates

At roughly one-day horizons, yesterday's strongest winners tend to underperform and yesterday's largest losers tend to recover.

But the effect is overwhelmingly concentrated in high-volatility stocks.

In our volatility split, the highest-volatility tercile produced approximately a −1.49% winner-minus-loser spread, while the middle and low-volatility groups were close to zero.

More importantly, once conditioned on high volatility, this short-term reversal became one of the most era-robust findings in the entire program.

This matters because an unconditional analysis can make the effect appear unstable.

The conditioned analysis suggests something more precise:

Short-term reversal is not universally unstable; it is concentrated in a particular type of security state.

Its symmetry and concentration in high-volatility names are consistent with explanations such as liquidity provision and bid-ask effects, although our data does not establish that mechanism causally.

Intermediate horizons: reversal becomes asymmetric

At approximately 5 to 20 day lookbacks and 20 to 60 day forward horizons, reversal remained visible, but its structure changed.

Large upward moves reversed substantially more aggressively than large downward moves.

And something even more interesting happened when we examined trend quality.

Smooth, efficient trends reversed more strongly than choppy moves.

At one specification, high-efficiency moves produced roughly −7.7%, compared with roughly −1.5% among low-efficiency moves.

That challenges a common intuition that a smooth trend must necessarily contain “higher-quality” information.

Sometimes the cleaner-looking move is precisely the one that later reverses hardest.

Medium horizons: momentum exists, but it is narrower than the slogan

The classical momentum region appeared around 60 to 120 day formation and holding horizons.

But momentum was far from uniform across securities.

It was disproportionately concentrated in extreme winners.

For example, the extreme-winner decile at 120 days showed approximately +14.5% continuation over the following 120 days in the underlying cross-sectional study.

The effect strengthened further among specific groups: low-volatility winners, high-efficiency trends, securities near their 52-week highs, high-relative-volume names and movements with large idiosyncratic components.

One particularly interesting result was the relationship with volatility.

Short-term reversal was strongest among the most volatile stocks.

Medium-term momentum was strongest among the calmest winners.

The same variable, volatility, helped separate two very different parts of the return-memory map.

But momentum also carried an important warning.

When the market entered high-volatility decline states, continuation could become reversal.

And even after conditioning on observable market states, some historical eras still behaved differently.

So we cannot reasonably describe momentum as a permanent market law that simply switches “on” during bull markets and “off” during stress.

There appears to be a slower structural component as well.

Why this phenomenon matters

A researcher building a momentum or mean-reversion model should therefore not begin with:

“Does momentum work?”

A better starting point is:

At which lookback, forward horizon, volatility state, market state, position in the historical range and source of movement does continuation or reversal exist?

That is a completely different research problem.

And usually a much more useful one.

Phenomenon 2: Volatility Clustering

“Volatility clusters” is one of the best-known stylized facts in finance.

Calm tends to follow calm.

Turbulence tends to follow turbulence.

Our data strongly confirms that statement.

But it also revealed one of the most important distinctions in the entire project.

Persistence is not necessarily memory.

Suppose one group of securities is structurally volatile while another group is structurally stable.

If the volatile stocks remain volatile next month, a standard persistence statistic will report enormous “volatility clustering.”

But perhaps nothing actually persisted through time.

Perhaps the identities of volatile companies simply remained the same.

We call that distinction:

Personality versus state.

To separate the two, we developed what became one of the program's most important controls: the personality floor, or shuffle floor.

The idea is simple.

Within each security, destroy the time ordering of the outcome while preserving that security's long-run distribution.

Whatever predictive structure survives cannot represent genuine temporal state persistence.

It represents persistent cross-sectional personality.

The result surprised us.

At approximately a 20-day horizon, raw volatility persistence measured around 0.744 rank IC.

But approximately 70% of that apparent persistence was attributable to permanent per-security personality.

At a one-year horizon, approximately 87% was personality.

The genuine time-varying state component survived.

It was real.

But it was closer to one-third of what the raw headline statistic suggested.

That distinction becomes extremely important when designing models.

Variance was dramatically easier to predict than direction

Perhaps the cleanest finding of the volatility module was this:

The size of future movement was 23× to 354× more predictable than its direction.

Using the same stocks, same dates and comparable methods, forward movement produced rank information coefficients around 0.62 to 0.77.

Directional prediction sat approximately between −0.016 and +0.034.

This distinction recurred independently in later modules.

The market often gives meaningful information about how much movement may occur while providing far less information about which direction that movement will take.

That is an extremely important distinction for quantitative researchers.

Risk forecasting and directional alpha generation are fundamentally different problems.

Calm can be more persistent than chaos

Another result challenged a common intuition.

Low-volatility states persisted longer than high-volatility states.

The estimated half-life was approximately 15.5 trading bars for high-volatility states and around 28 bars for low-volatility states.

In other words:

Calm was stickier than storm.

Volatility spikes also decayed faster when their volatility was concentrated in a few extreme days than when similar volatility accumulated gradually.

A spike can be more forgettable than a grind.

Complexity did not automatically win

We also compared volatility estimators.

GARCH performed well on the raw persistence statistic.

But after removing the personality floor, it performed worse on the state component than simpler alternatives.

EWMA, with no fitted parameters, produced the strongest tested state estimate.

This is an important research lesson beyond volatility:

A more sophisticated model can win the wrong metric.

Phenomenon 3: Jumps and Shocks

Suppose a stock moves 8% in a single session.

What should we expect afterward?

Continuation?

Reversal?

Higher volatility?

A different outcome if the move occurred overnight rather than intraday?

We studied more than 321,000 shock events.

And once again, the headline result became much smaller after we asked where it actually came from.

Raw post-shock underperformance at 20 bars was approximately −0.51%.

But roughly 63% of that was associated with ticker personality.

Near-shocks, observations just below the event threshold, behaved surprisingly similarly.

After the relevant controls, our estimated event-attributable excess was closer to −0.19%, roughly 37% of the original headline.

That teaches an important lesson about event studies:

Researchers create thresholds. Markets often do not.

There was no magical boundary where a “shock” suddenly became a different economic object.

The behavior transitioned through a continuum.

The most reliable post-shock information concerned movement, not direction

Residual price impact predicted substantially more future movement.

But it did not robustly predict which direction prices would move.

Gap-led versus intraday shocks changed the subsequent amount of movement and the probability of complete retracement, but not direction in a clean way.

Own-history abnormality added substantial information for forward volatility and much less for directional outcomes.

Across several independent constructions, the same message kept returning:

Impact is a movement variable more reliably than a direction variable.

That dovetails almost perfectly with the volatility module.

Different methodology.

Different research question.

Same broader conclusion.

Shocks cluster too

Shock events clustered beyond what ordinary volatility clustering alone could explain over short horizons.

Interestingly, that excess disappeared and even reversed after approximately 20 days.

That timescale aligned closely with the volatility-state half-lives identified independently in Phenomenon 2.

Independent modules beginning from different questions were beginning to point toward the same market timescale.

That is much more interesting to us than finding one isolated statistically significant coefficient.

And one of our best-looking historical conclusions was completely wrong

An early result suggested:

“Shock impact has doubled since 2005.”

It would have made an excellent headline.

It was withdrawn.

The entire trend came from changes in the eligible universe.

After correcting the universe comparison, the series was essentially flat.

This is exactly the kind of result a survivorship-biased or historically inconsistent dataset can publish with high statistical confidence.

The mathematics can be correct.

The inference can still be wrong.

Phenomenon 4: Volume and Liquidity

Volume is one of the most frequently interpreted variables in discretionary and systematic trading.

“High volume confirms the move.”

“Low volume means weak conviction.”

“Large volume with little price movement means absorption.”

“Liquidity shocks reveal informed trading.”

Many of these stories sound plausible.

We wanted to know which of them were actually measurable.

The strongest evidence concerned volatility.

Relative volume predicted forward realized volatility beyond its personality floor.

Residualized price impact also predicted forward volatility and remained remarkably stable across eras.

The relationship with signed return existed as well, but it was much smaller.

Again:

Activity tells us considerably more about future movement than future direction.

Liquidity state turned out to matter more than liquidity level

This became one of the most important distinctions in the module.

The structural level of illiquidity did not robustly support the hypothesis we had originally written.

But deviations from normal liquidity, the current liquidity state, persisted extremely strongly.

At a 20-day horizon, liquidity-state persistence above the personality floor measured approximately +0.468.

Unlike volatility, this was one of the rare places in the program where the dynamic state component dominated the permanent personality component.

Even more importantly, looking only at raw information coefficients would have produced the opposite ranking.

Raw statistics made liquidity level appear stronger than state.

After removing personality, state became roughly twice as persistent as level at a 60-day horizon.

This is one of the clearest examples of why we do not want to rank candidate features by raw predictive strength alone.

You can select the wrong variable with a perfectly legitimate statistic.

Some attractive stories could not be tested

Several popular interpretations required information our dataset did not contain.

True bid-ask spread required better quote information.

Turnover required reliable shares-outstanding history.

Absorption required signed order flow.

Instead of manufacturing approximations and then telling a mechanism story around them, those questions were labeled NOT TESTABLE.

That may sound less exciting than producing another chart.

But “we cannot answer this with the available data” is a valid quantitative result.

Sometimes it is the only scientifically defensible one.

Phenomenon 5: Market Resiliency and Impact Decay

After a large price displacement, does price come back?

How quickly?

Does activity normalize before price?

Can we rank securities by their “resiliency”?

This module was heavily pre-registered because several of these questions are particularly vulnerable to researcher discretion.

Five hypotheses were declared before the results.

Only two rejected their nulls.

The largest substantive result was straightforward:

Trading activity normalized much faster than price displacement.

The weaker statistical arm produced a t-statistic of approximately +80.9.

Price itself did not show an equally universal retracement pattern.

Upward and downward displacements behaved differently enough that the general hypothesis of uniform 20-day decay failed.

That distinction matters.

A market can recover operationally, with trading activity returning toward normal, without price returning to its previous level.

Statistical significance was not practical predictability

Another hypothesis asked whether day-zero features could predict whether a displaced price would ultimately be “accepted.”

The relationship passed the pre-registered statistical rule.

But it explained only approximately 0.2 to 0.3% of the variance.

So the correct conclusion was deliberately awkward:

Strong statistical evidence of a negligible effect.

Both pieces matter.

A relationship can be statistically real and practically useless.

Conflating those two questions is one of the easiest ways to transform empirical research into marketing.

Then came one of our most instructive false positives

We asked whether resiliency itself was a persistent stock characteristic.

Initially, the result looked strong.

Split-half reliability was approximately +0.259 for upward events and +0.339 for downward events.

Relative to the original shuffle floor, the result looked overwhelmingly significant.

It was an artifact.

Events within the same security occurred close together.

Two events separated by only three sessions shared 17 of their 20 forward observations.

Randomly splitting those events into two groups therefore created correlation between the groups by construction.

The shuffle control did not remove the relevant overlap.

Once events were thinned so their forward windows no longer overlapped, reliability collapsed to approximately −0.047 and −0.051.

The estimated trait variance became negative.

The positive headline disappeared.

The final verdict:

Resiliency was not supported as a stable stock trait.

More importantly, we encoded the failure into the research infrastructure.

The reusable resiliency toolkit now refuses to run certain reliability analyses on overlapping events unless the researcher explicitly overrides the guard and records a justification.

That is an important part of how we think research systems should evolve.

A research mistake should not merely produce a corrected PDF.

It should change the machinery so the same mistake becomes harder to make again.

Phenomenon 6: Common vs. Idiosyncratic Movement

By this point, several modules had suggested an interesting theme.

Stock-specific movement often appeared different from market-driven movement.

In Phenomenon 1, idiosyncratic share organized both short-term reversal and medium-term continuation.

In the shock study, idiosyncratic and common shocks behaved differently in several specifications.

In the liquidity work, predictive information appeared concentrated in ticker-specific rather than date-common components.

So Phenomenon 6 was designed specifically to challenge that idea.

And it did.

The strongest result was that commonality itself persists.

Across 480 specifications, 439 survived false-discovery-rate control, with a median absolute effect around 0.398.

How much of a stock's movement is market-driven appears to be a persistent characteristic.

Commonality also tended to rise during crises, although that effect was much smaller.

But the more exciting hypothesis, that common versus idiosyncratic movement broadly determines future reversal or retention, did not generalize nearly as well.

Reversal-by-source survived only 15 of 3,240 specifications.

Retention-by-source survived only 8 of 3,240.

Those effects existed in particular regimes and specifications.

They were not broad laws.

This created one of the most useful unresolved tensions in the entire program.

Several earlier modules found idiosyncratic share highly informative.

The module built specifically to test that idea exhaustively found that it did not generalize.

We are not resolving that contradiction rhetorically.

It remains unresolved.

And that is exactly how research should work.

A new study is not obligated to confirm the story created by the previous study.

Its job is to attack it.

Phenomenon 7: Correlation, Dispersion and Crowding

The seventh module produced perhaps the most important negative result of the program.

Average pairwise correlation, dispersion and principal-component concentration are widely used as market-state variables.

Their rolling charts often look persistent.

But rolling measurements introduce a serious statistical problem.

Imagine calculating a 120-day correlation every day.

Today's estimate uses observations 1 through 120.

Tomorrow's estimate uses observations 2 through 121.

Those two measurements share 119 of 120 inputs.

They are almost the same observation.

Yet a naive statistical test can treat each day as independent.

We tested what happened when inference was corrected for that dependence.

Across six persistence and concentration families:

0 of 155 tests survived robust overlap-aware significance.

A representative example was average 120-day correlation at a one-day horizon.

The naive p-value was effectively:

0.000

The robust p-value became:

0.888

For 120-day PC1 concentration, the corresponding robust p-value was approximately:

0.924

That is not a minor adjustment.

It is the difference between apparent certainty and no defensible evidence.

But correlation was not “useless”

This distinction is critical.

The persistence forecasts failed.

Contemporaneous state descriptions did not.

Contrast families describing combinations of correlation, dispersion, shocks and unwind-like conditions produced hundreds of robust survivors.

So our conclusion is not:

“Correlation does not matter.”

It is much narrower:

We found far stronger evidence that correlation and dispersion describe the market state we are currently in than that their rolling levels can be reliably forecast using naive persistence logic.

That is a much less sensational statement.

It is also much more useful.

Phenomenon 7 currently remains under external review.

Seven modules have been executed.

Six are closed.

The seventh remains held at the gate, and the next module remains blocked behind it.

That delay is intentional.

A research gate that never prevents the next stage from starting is not much of a gate.

What the seven phenomena taught us together

The value of the program is not seven isolated sets of statistics.

It is what repeatedly appeared across independent investigations.

1. Much of what looks like market memory is actually market personality

This appeared in volatility.

It appeared after shocks.

It appeared in liquidity.

It appeared in commonality.

Before interpreting persistence as a dynamic state, we should ask:

Does the variable persist, or do the same securities simply remain high or low on that variable?

Those are very different economic claims.

The personality-floor framework has consequently become one of the first controls we want to consider whenever a cross-sectional variable appears persistent.

2. Markets appear considerably more predictable in magnitude than in direction

Volatility predicted volatility.

Volume predicted volatility.

Price impact predicted future movement.

Shock abnormality predicted movement.

Direction was repeatedly smaller, weaker or less stable.

This does not imply directional alpha is impossible.

It means researchers should not treat forecasting direction and forecasting uncertainty as equivalent problems.

The data certainly did not.

3. “Common versus idiosyncratic” matters, but not as cleanly as we initially hoped

Several modules independently found strong differences.

Then the dedicated module showed those differences were highly specification-dependent.

That is not a failure of the program.

It is exactly why the program exists.

A compelling explanation discovered in one setting should become a hypothesis to attack in the next.

4. The inference method can decide whether a phenomenon exists

In the shock study, naive inference inflated a t-statistic by approximately 3.3×.

In the liquidity work, two-way clustering inflated standard errors by approximately 2.16× relative to naive inference.

In the correlation study, correcting overlapping observations took entire families from almost universal significance to 0 of 155 survivors.

In the resiliency study, eliminating overlapping event windows destroyed what had looked like a stable security trait.

At the same time, volatility clustering survived its own overlap test almost unchanged.

That last observation matters enormously.

Robust inference is not a machine for eliminating results.

It is a way to learn which results survive.

5. Self-correction is part of the research output

Across the program, thirteen previously reported findings were withdrawn, reversed, blocked or materially restated.

Some corrections removed positive results.

One correction actually restored a statistically significant result because the original grading had used a criterion that had never been pre-registered.

That symmetry matters.

The objective cannot be to make results weaker.

The objective has to be to make them correct.

A research process that never changes its mind should make us nervous.

If hundreds or thousands of tests have been run and every conclusion survives exactly as originally written, that may indicate exceptional work.

It may also indicate that nobody seriously tried to break it.

What happens if we skip this layer of research?

This is where the framework becomes practical.

Imagine building a strategy on a variable that shows strong persistence.

Without a personality control, we may believe we discovered a tradable state when we actually discovered that certain stocks permanently rank higher on that variable.

Imagine discovering a post-event anomaly.

Without a point-in-time universe, we may attribute historical underperformance to the event when much of it comes from changing universe composition.

Imagine discovering a rolling correlation signal.

Without overlap-aware inference, we may report extraordinary statistical certainty because 119 of 120 observations are being repeatedly counted.

Imagine using volume and price impact to argue that institutional “absorption” is occurring.

Without signed order-flow data, we have moved from measurement into storytelling.

Imagine producing a beautifully significant coefficient.

Without separating statistical significance from economic magnitude, a relationship explaining 0.2% of the variance can be presented as meaningful predictability.

And imagine a hypothesis failing.

Without checking whether the pipeline actually generated the tests we think it generated, an empty filter can look exactly like a null result.

That last failure happened in our own work.

A filtering condition referenced a value that no upstream stage ever produced.

The filter returned nothing.

Nothing crashed.

Nothing looked obviously broken.

The output simply looked like “no evidence.”

Meanwhile, thousands of relevant tests were sitting elsewhere in the same inference table.

These are the failures that worry us most.

Not the obvious exceptions.

Not the red error message.

The dangerous failures are the ones that generate a perfectly formatted table.

How we now think about quantitative research

The seven phenomena have gradually turned into a reusable sequence of questions that can be applied well beyond these specific studies:

  1. What is the phenomenon? Define the economic behavior before defining the strategy. Is the hypothesis about continuation, reversal, uncertainty, liquidity, impact, resiliency, commonality or market state?
  2. What part is permanent personality and what part is changing state? Construct the appropriate floor or null before interpreting persistence.
  3. What actually carries information: direction, magnitude, path or state? Do not force a directional interpretation onto a movement signal.
  4. Where did the movement come from? Separate market-wide, sector/common and security-specific components where the data allows it.
  5. What happens after the event? Measure continuation, retracement, volatility, liquidity normalization and acceptance independently rather than treating “reaction” as one outcome.
  6. What inference structure does the data require? Audit overlapping windows, repeated securities, repeated dates, multiple hypotheses, changing universes and dependence before trusting the p-value.
  7. What would make us withdraw the finding? Decide the falsification tests, failure thresholds and research gates before becoming attached to the result.

Only after those questions survive should the conversation move naturally toward portfolio construction, execution, transaction costs, capacity, risk management and ultimately a strategy.

That order matters.

From signal hunting to market understanding

There is a strong temptation in quantitative finance to optimize too early.

A variable produces a promising result.

We add a filter.

Performance improves.

We add another condition.

Sharpe increases.

Soon we have a strategy with excellent historical statistics and very little understanding of the mechanism underneath it.

The seven-phenomena program has pushed us in the opposite direction.

We are increasingly interested in making the underlying question harder before making the strategy more complicated.

When momentum appears, ask where.

When volatility persists, ask how much is personality.

When a shock predicts something, ask whether it predicts direction or only movement.

When volume carries information, ask whether it is level or state.

When price is displaced, ask what actually recovers.

When idiosyncratic movement appears special, build another study whose purpose is to prove that it is not.

When correlation looks persistent, ask how many genuinely independent observations created that conclusion.

And when a finding disappears under those tests, keep the disappearance.

Because a null result that prevents a false model from reaching production can be more valuable than another attractive backtest.

The broader objective at Light Water

We do not view these seven phenomena as seven finished answers.

We view them as pieces of research infrastructure.

The return-memory map helps us understand where continuation and reversal live.

The volatility work forces us to distinguish persistent securities from persistent states.

The shock framework separates event effects from the continuum surrounding them.

The liquidity work separates trading activity from the stories we instinctively tell about it.

The resiliency framework forces price recovery and activity recovery to be measured independently.

The common-versus-idiosyncratic work tests whether a signal belongs to the market or the security.

And the correlation work forces market-state models to survive the dependence created by the measurements themselves.

Together, these become a foundation that future strategy research can sit on.

A momentum model can now be interrogated with the volatility and market-state framework.

A reversal model can be checked against shock behavior and liquidity conditions.

A breakout model can be investigated through continuation, commonality, activity and correlation.

A regime system can be evaluated against what our data says about volatility persistence and crowding.

The purpose is not to turn every research project into a five-year academic exercise.

It is the opposite.

Once the foundational machinery exists, many bad ideas can be rejected faster.

And promising ideas can be tested more intelligently.

Research should explain the “why” before optimizing the “what”

Quantitative finance will always involve uncertainty.

We will never eliminate regime changes.

We will never perfectly identify every mechanism.

And daily market data cannot answer questions that fundamentally require an order book, signed flow or intraday reconstruction.

The goal is not certainty.

The goal is to know exactly where the uncertainty begins.

That is perhaps the most important thing these seven investigations have taught us.

The backbone of quantitative research is not a collection of indicators.

It is a process for separating:

Observation from explanation,

state from personality,

movement from direction,

correlation from causation,

statistical significance from economic usefulness,

and a genuine market effect from an artifact of the research process itself.

Before building the strategy, understand the phenomenon.

Before optimizing the signal, understand what the signal measures.

Before explaining the result, try to destroy it.

And when it survives, understand why.

That is the research framework we are continuing to build at Light Water.

Not because it guarantees that the next strategy will work.

But because it makes it considerably harder to convince ourselves that something works when it does not.

This article describes empirical research into historical U.S. equity-market behavior. The phenomena discussed here are research findings, not trading strategies, investment recommendations, or claims of implementable returns. No strategy-level transaction costs, execution assumptions, position sizing, capacity estimates or portfolio backtests are implied by the statistics discussed above.
