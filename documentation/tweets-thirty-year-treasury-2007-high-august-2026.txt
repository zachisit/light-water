The 30-year Treasury hit 5.33% this week — highest since 2007.

Everyone's blaming the Fed.

The Fed didn't move. And the 2-year yield went the other way.

That one detail changes the entire read. 🧵

2/
Any long yield is two things stacked:

→ the average short rate you expect over 30 years
→ the term premium — what you demand for eating 30 years of uncertainty

The 2-year is almost purely the first piece.

3/
In August: the 2-year fell ~12bp. The 30-year rose ~13bp. Fed funds unchanged at 3.50–3.75%.

If this were a Fed repricing, both ends move together.

They didn't. So the move came from the term premium.

This is a fiscal price, not a Fed price.

4/
The supply math isn't subtle:

• ~$2.1T projected FY26 deficit
• $857B interest expense in 9 months, +13% y/y
• The Aug 13 auction cleared at 5.216% — priciest long-bond sale in ~25 years

More paper. Same buyers. Someone has to be paid to hold it.

5/
And there's a second borrower that didn't exist in this form last cycle.

Hyperscalers issued ~$121B of corporate bonds in 2025.
Projected near $230B net this year.

The AI buildout is being financed in the bond market — competing for the same finite appetite for duration.

6/
Now the part most bondholders have never actually calculated.

A 30-year Treasury has a modified duration near 15.

One percentage point against you = about -13.4% of principal.

Two and a half years of coupon. Gone.

7/
Which means the "safe" sleeve of the portfolio is, in price terms, one of the most volatile things on a brokerage statement.

TLT's equity-like drawdowns since 2020 weren't a malfunction.

That's the math working exactly as designed.

8/
The encouraging half of the same math:

A one-point rally is worth ~+16.8%. Gains beat losses — that gap is convexity, and it grows with duration.

You're paid 5.3% to wait.

The question was never whether the long bond is attractive. It's whether you're sized for the path.
