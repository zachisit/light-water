Nineteen Years

The 30-year Treasury topped 5.33% this week — the highest since 2007. The detail almost nobody is saying out loud: the two-year yield went the other way. It fell about 12 basis points in August while the long bond rose 13, and the Fed never moved from 3.50–3.75%. That combination rules out the easy explanation. A long yield is two things — the average short rate you expect over thirty years, and the term premium you demand for accepting thirty years of uncertainty. The two-year is almost entirely the first piece, and it went down. So the move came from the second. This is a fiscal price, not a Fed price.

The arithmetic behind it isn't subtle: a projected $2.1 trillion deficit for fiscal 2026, $857 billion of interest expense in nine months, and a 13 August auction that cleared at 5.216% — the most expensive long-bond sale in about twenty-five years. There is also a second borrower that did not exist in this form last cycle. Hyperscalers issued roughly $121 billion of corporate bonds in 2025 and are projected near $230 billion net this year, competing for the same finite appetite for duration.

Which brings up the number most bondholders have never run. A thirty-year Treasury has a modified duration near 15 — one percentage point against you costs about 13.4% of principal, roughly two and a half years of coupon. The instrument people reach for when they want to be conservative is one of the most volatile things on a brokerage statement. The encouraging half of that same math: a one-point rally is worth about 16.8%, and you are being paid 5.3% to wait. The question was never whether the long bond is attractive. It is whether you are sized for the path.
